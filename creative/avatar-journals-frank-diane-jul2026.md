## FRANK — Tuesday, July 14, 2026

Katie gave me this notebook two Christmases ago and I have maybe thirty
pages of it filled, which averages out to about a page a month, which is
about right for a man who spent forty years writing everything in
specification language. She said write like nobody's reading. Nobody is
reading. That's the only reason I can do it at all.

Walked the loop this morning. 2.4 miles, out by the Hendersons' and up the
hill that I have started to think of as the test bench. I don't tell anyone
that's what I call it. Here is what I do now, and I don't remember deciding
to start doing it: I listen. The whole way up that hill I'm listening to my
own chest the way I used to listen to a machine that had thrown an
intermittent fault — not looking at it, not touching it, just aware of it
the way you're aware of a sound that shouldn't be there. Forty years I
trusted instruments. Pressure gauges, torque specs, vibration meters. You
don't guess with a machine, you measure. And here I am at sixty years old
walking up a hill with the only machine I can't put a gauge on, listening
like some caveman with his ear against the ground.

Made it up fine. I always make it up fine. That's the thing — I feel fine.
Dave felt fine too, and I've decided I hate that word more than any word in
the English language, but I'll get to Dave or I won't.

Diane made oatmeal again. Third time this week. There was a time that woman
made biscuits and sausage gravy on a Tuesday for no reason at all, and now
it's steel-cut oats with walnuts, and the butter has quietly become olive
oil, and the salt shaker has quietly moved from the table to the cabinet,
and nobody has said one word about any of it. I know exactly what she's
doing. She knows I know. And we eat the oatmeal in the warm kitchen and talk
about Tommy's swim lessons, and the one thing we do not talk about sits in
the middle of the table like a centerpiece. I eat every bite. I want to
tell her: I see you. I see what you're doing and I love you for it and it
makes me feel like a patient in my own kitchen. But if I say that out loud,
then we're talking about it. And if we're talking about it, then it's real,
and there's a doctor's name in it, and a scan with a number on it, and I'm
not ready to be a number. My father became a number in the driveway when I
was twenty-seven. I remember the EMT saying it to somebody — a number, his
age — like that explained everything.

Took my pill with the orange juice. Little white pill. I've taken it every
morning for three years and I could not tell you one thing it has actually
done. That's what gets me — not the pill itself, the flying blind. "It
should be helping." Should. I ran a shop for thirty years; if a man on my
floor told me a bearing "should" be holding, I'd have him pull the housing
and show me. Nobody ever shows me anything. The physical in April took
eleven minutes, I timed it. Looks good, numbers look fine, see you next
year, and I sat in the truck in that parking lot for I'm going to say ten
minutes, keys in my hand, engine off, and the thought I could not get rid of
was: based on what? What did you look at? You listened to a heart for
fifteen seconds through a shirt. I test deeper than that on a lawnmower.

I didn't go back in and ask. That's the part I'd only write here. I wanted
to and I didn't, because a man who goes back in and asks is a man who's
worried, and I couldn't stand for the girl at the desk to see that.

Stopped at the supply house before lunch. Their counter's on the second
floor, fourteen steps, I've climbed them a couple hundred times. Today at
the top I needed a breath I didn't used to need. One breath. Maybe it was
nothing. It's July, it was ninety-one degrees, I'd had two cups of coffee.
I logged it anyway — that's the honest word for what I do now, I log these
things, in my head, like fault codes. The flutter in June that was probably
the coffee. The breath at the top of the stairs. None of it has repeated on
demand. Any mechanic will tell you the intermittent faults are the ones
that kill you, because they never show up when the machine's on the bench.

I have not told Diane about the stairs, or June. Write it down, Frank, look
at it: there are things going on in my body I have not told my wife. If she
did that to me I'd be furious. I do it to her every day and I call it
protecting her.

Ate lunch in the truck. Almost played Dave's voicemail again. Forty-seven
seconds, still saved, ten months now. I know it by heart anyway — he wasn't
even calling about anything, tee time and a joke about my slice, that's the
whole message, that's the last of him. We played eighteen holes that
Saturday and he bought the beers and he was dead on Wednesday and every
single person at that funeral said the same sentence, and I stood there in
my gray suit wanting to put my fist through the wall every time: he seemed
fine. He was sixty-one. I turn sixty-one in October. My father made it to
sixty-three. I'm an engineer; I know three points don't make a curve. I
also know I check my 401(k) every single morning — charts on that thing
going back to 1987, I can tell you what my money did on any Tuesday of my
life. I cannot tell you one thing my arteries have ever done. Not one data
point. Retirement's all funded and I've started wondering, quietly, in the
truck, at lunch, who I'm funding it for.

Diane sent me another video Sunday. Doctor on YouTube, older guy, talks
slow, no yelling. I tell her nothing and usually I watch nothing. Today I
watched four minutes of it in the truck with the AC running. He said the
test I keep getting isn't wrong, it's answering the wrong question. I
turned it off there. Not because it was stupid. Because it wasn't. Four
minutes in and the man had put words on the exact thing I sat in that
parking lot feeling in April, and I wasn't ready for what minute five might
be. I didn't tell her I watched it. If I tell her, it becomes hers again —
the whole thing becomes her project, and I become the project, and I am so
tired of being managed by people who won't show me the data. That's not
fair to her. I know it's not fair. She's not managing me, she's terrified.
I know the difference. Writing it down doesn't fix it.

Dinner was good. I didn't salt the potatoes, and I saw her see it. Tommy
video after — that boy on the swing, screaming laughing. I want his high
school graduation. I did the math tonight like I do: 2038. I want Portugal
with Diane, the trip we keep not booking. I want to be a boring old man.
That's the whole want. Boring, and old.

She's asleep now. She thinks I don't know she listens to my breathing.
Sometimes I breathe deep and even on purpose so she'll go to sleep first.
That's a thing I do for love and it is also a lie, and I don't know what to
do with the fact that both are true.

I'm not afraid of dying. Write it plain: that's not the fear. The fear is
her finding me. The fear is the kitchen floor and her hands on my chest
doing something she saw on television, and the last thing I ever gave her
being that. The fear is that they'd all stand in the funeral home and say
he seemed fine, and they'd be right, because seeming fine is the only thing
I've been doing about any of this.

Tomorrow: walk the hill, listen, say nothing. Same plan as today.

*— F.*

## DIANE — Tuesday, July 14, 2026

Eleven steps from our bedroom to the kitchen. He paused on the ninth this
morning. One beat, hand on the rail, and then the coffee maker and the
newspaper sounds, everything ordinary. I've become a woman who counts her
husband's stairs. I never decided to become her; she just showed up
somewhere around the time of Dave's funeral and she has been running this
house ever since.

I watched him take his pill from behind the refrigerator door, which is
where I do my watching, and I felt the two things I always feel at 7:40 in
the morning: thank God, and it's not enough. It's a little white pill and a
prayer, that's our whole program. Nobody has ever looked inside my husband.
Sixty years old, a father who died in a driveway at sixty-three, a best
friend ten months in the ground, and the sum total of what medicine has
ever actually looked at is some numbers from his arm and fifteen seconds
with a stethoscope. "His numbers look fine." I have learned enough at one
in the morning to know exactly how little that sentence means, and I have
to live inside it anyway, because his doctor said it, and who am I? I'm
just the woman who would find him.

I made the oatmeal again. Third time this week — I ration it so it doesn't
look like a regimen, the same way I ration everything now. I cook like a
woman defusing a bomb she isn't allowed to admit exists. Olive oil where
the butter was, salmon on Thursdays, the salt shaker gone to the cabinet
like it just wandered off. He knows. Of course he knows — the man notices a
quarter-turn on a bolt; he noticed the butter. And he eats every bite and
says it's good and we talk about Tommy's swim lessons, and the whole time
there's a second conversation happening at that table with no words in it
at all. Some mornings I think that silent conversation is the most honest
one we have. Some mornings I think it's killing us both a little, having it
in silence, both of us, every single day, being so careful of each other.

Kroger after breakfast. I read labels now the way I used to read novels —
sodium, saturated fat, I stand in the bread aisle doing arithmetic for a
man who isn't there. Ran into Carol by the pharmacy. Bill's eight months
out from his stent and she looked — I'm going to write the true word —
peaceful. She has a follow-up schedule. A cardiologist's cell number. A
list on the refrigerator. I stood there holding my low-sodium everything
and I envied her, I envied a woman whose husband has heart disease, because
her fear has a name and a doctor attached to it, and mine just has a
weather report. At least Carol knows what she's fighting. I'm fighting fog.
I drove home and cried in our driveway for about ninety seconds, fixed my
face in the mirror, and carried the groceries in. That's a skill I have
now. Ninety-second grief, then groceries.

It has been nine days since I last said the words "calcium score" out
loud. Yes, I count. I keep an economy of it — I get so many mentions a
month before his jaw does the thing and the shutters come down, so I spend
them like they're the last money we have. Nine days ago I spent one and got
"my numbers are fine, Diane" and eleven steps of silence, and so today,
when he came home and I saw he was tired around the eyes, I asked "you
okay?" — one nag's worth of currency, small denomination — and he said "I'm
fine." Fine. I have come to hate that word the way other women hate another
woman's name. Dave was fine. Everyone at that funeral said it like a hymn —
he seemed fine, he seemed fine — and my husband stood there in his gray
suit with his hands in fists and I watched him decide, I watched him decide
right there at the casket, to be fine too.

Katie called at four. She got as far as "how's Dad" and I got as far as the
breath before "would you talk to—" and I stopped myself. It's not fair to
make our daughter the messenger, and it doesn't work anyway — it just makes
him feel ganged up on, ambushed in his own family. So I said Dad's fine.
Now I'm doing it too. That word is contagious. It's the official language
of this house and we both speak it fluently and neither of us has believed
a word of it in about a year.

Found his watch on the dresser this afternoon — the one I gave him for
Christmas, four hundred dollars, heart rate, oxygen, everything. He uses it
to check the weather. I stood there and laughed out loud at the most
expensive weather station in Ohio, and then I wasn't laughing. He wears my
gift and won't open my gift. That's the whole marriage right now, if I'm
honest. He'll take the oatmeal, wear the watch, swallow the pill — he'll
accept every soft thing, as long as nobody says the hard thing out loud.

Here is what I would only write here. I have planned his funeral. Not on
paper — in the 2 a.m. theater behind my eyes, complete: the gray suit, the
second hymn, which of his shop friends would carry him. I hate myself every
time the curtain opens on it, and I can't always stop it, and I have never
told one living soul. And in March I took the CPR class at the community
center — Tuesday mornings, four weeks, I told him it was a library thing. I
have a card in my wallet that says I know how to try to save my husband's
life, and it lives behind my library card, hidden, because the day he finds
it is the day he learns his wife is studying for his death. I refuse to
make that class my retirement plan. And I renewed the card in the spring.
Both things are true and I don't know how to hold them.

Tonight, 1:30, kitchen, tea going cold. The tabs again. I know more about
a man's heart at one in the morning than anyone has ever told my husband in
an exam room, and I never wanted any of it — I wanted to be reading a novel
next to him with my cold feet under his leg. There's a doctor I keep coming
back to. The older one, the slow talker. Johns Hopkins, forty years, and he
doesn't yell and he doesn't sell panic and he says things like *lifestyle
first, medication when it's needed* — a grown-up, is what he sounds like.
He sounds, honestly, like the man Frank would have listened to his whole
life: an engineer's kind of doctor. Show your work. I sent one of his
videos Sunday. "Worth 10 minutes ♥." He never watches them. I know he never
watches them, and I sat there tonight with my thumb over the next one
doing the arithmetic I always do — too many and I'm nagging, too few and
I'm not trying — and I saved it for Thursday. Thursday feels casual.
Casual is the only door he leaves unlocked.

What do I want? People think the wives want obedience — eat this, go there.
I want to be his wife again, that's all. I want to stop being the ninth-
step counter, the label reader, the funeral rehearser. I want him to walk
in from the truck one day holding his phone and say "Di, watch this with
me" — him to me, four words. I would give this house away for those four
words. I want a number, even a bad number. Give me a bad number and a plan
and I will cook for that plan and walk for that plan and I will never doom-
scroll again as long as I live. It's not the disease that's eating this
marriage. It's the not-looking.

I'm going back up now. I'll lie down next to him and put my hand where I
can feel his back rise, and count a few, and lie to myself that I'm not
counting. His breathing was deep and even tonight, from the minute I came
up the stairs — so even it was almost like a performance.

Sometimes I think he does it for me.

Sometimes I almost let myself wonder what that means.

*— D.*
