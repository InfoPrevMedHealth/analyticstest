# Diane 2.0 — Build-a-Better-Buyer Persona + Parallel Campaign Plan — July 2026

The night-researcher avatar (Pain Point #21) run through the full buyer-
persona framework: Target Market = the researching spouse/adult child of an
at-risk man; Product = Dr. Brewer's prevention content and programs, entering
through the free video funnel. This is **internal research material** — fears,
outcomes, and second-person language are allowed here and must NOT migrate
into ad copy without passing the prime-directive gate. The compliant ad
translations are at the end.

---

## Demographic

**Name:** Diane
**Age:** 61 (range 55–68)
**Brief Description:** Married to Frank for 34 years. Retired or semi-retired
(teacher, nurse, office manager — a career that trained her to notice
things). Two grown kids, one grandchild. She is the household's health
quarterback: books the appointments, fills the fridge, remembers that
Frank's father died at 63. On Facebook daily; runs the family group chat.
Her search history after midnight is a love letter written in fear.

## Core Problem

**The Core Problem She Faces:** She can see a risk her husband refuses to
look at, and she carries the fear for both of them — alone, and without the
authority to make him act. Every tool she has (nagging, cooking, forwarding,
recruiting the kids) either bounces off his pride or damages the marriage
she's trying to protect.

**Top 5 Emotions Around That Problem:**
1. Dread — low-grade, constant, worst between 1 and 3 a.m.
2. Helplessness — she can research everything and compel nothing.
3. Quiet resentment — that she has to be the one who carries this.
4. Guilt — for nagging, and equally for the days she gives up and stays quiet.
5. Loneliness — the worry is a room nobody else enters.

**Top 5 Biggest Fears (the 3 a.m. ones she'd never say aloud):**
1. Finding him on the kitchen floor — being the one doing chest compressions
   while the ambulance is still eight minutes out.
2. That there was a window — a scan, a conversation, a right set of words —
   and she'll spend widowhood knowing she didn't find it in time.
3. That she has already rehearsed the funeral. She's caught herself
   imagining the empty side of the bed, selling the house, retirement alone
   — and she hates herself for it.
4. That he already knows something is wrong and is hiding it from her.
   (The exact mirror of the quarantined "haven't told my wife" block —
   these two people live in the same house.)
5. Not death but diminishment — becoming his nurse instead of his wife,
   pushing a wheelchair through the retirement they saved thirty years for.

**5 Ways Those Fears Affect Close Relationships:**
1. **Frank:** Her fear leaks out as surveillance — watching him salt his
   food, counting his beers, listening to him climb the stairs. He hears
   criticism, digs in harder, and now there are two silences at the dinner
   table: his symptoms and her worry.
2. **Her marriage's intimacy:** She monitors instead of connects. Some
   nights she stays awake to listen to his breathing. Vigilance has quietly
   replaced closeness — she touches his chest to check, not to hold.
3. **The kids:** She recruits them — "talk to your father" — and family
   calls turn into failed interventions. Her daughter now screens her calls
   half-expecting a health briefing.
4. **Friends:** She goes quiet when friends complain about kitchen
   renovations. She envies couples with boring health. She never says the
   real thing out loud because saying it would make it real.
5. **Herself:** Every ounce of health vigilance goes to him. She's two
   years overdue for her own mammogram. The quarterback has no coach.

**5 Conversational But Hurtful Things Those People Might Say:**
1. Frank: "You're not my doctor, Diane."
2. Frank: "I'm fine. The worrying is your problem, not mine."
3. Her son: "Mom, Dad's a grown man. Let him live his life."
4. Her sister: "You've been saying this for five years and nothing's
   happened."
5. Frank, ending the conversation: "My dad never went to doctors either."
   (Said as comfort. Heard as the family history repeating itself.)

## Other Solutions

**What She's Tried in the Past:**
1. Direct action — booking the physical herself, driving him there.
2. Stealth health — the quiet olive-oil swap, salmon twice a week, smaller
   plates, decaf after noon.
3. The forward — articles, YouTube clips, podcast episodes, "worth 10
   minutes ♥."
4. Recruiting authority — mentioning her concerns to his doctor before the
   visit; recruiting the kids for a united front.
5. Gadget diplomacy — the smartwatch for Christmas, the blood-pressure cuff
   that lives in a drawer.
6. Solo night research — Google, forums, YouTube rabbit holes; she knows
   what a calcium score is and he doesn't.

**Soundbites About What She's Tried:**
1. "I made the appointment. All he had to do was show up. He rescheduled
   it twice."
2. "I've cooked heart-healthy for a year. He orders wings with his buddies
   and thinks I don't know."
3. "I send him ten-minute videos. He watches fishing videos instead."
4. "I told his doctor about the stairs thing. The doctor asked HIM, and he
   said 'no, nothing.' What was I supposed to do — call him a liar in the
   exam room?"
5. "The watch was $400. He uses it to check the weather."
6. "By 1 a.m. I knew more about calcium scores than anyone had ever told
   him."

**What She Doesn't Want to Do to Fix Her Problem:**
1. Become his mother instead of his wife.
2. Push so hard he stops telling her anything at all.
3. Go behind his back in a way that breaks trust if discovered.
4. Sit and wait for the event her research says is coming.
5. Accept one more "his numbers look fine, let's watch and wait."

**Soundbites About What She Doesn't Want to Do:**
1. "I don't want to be his mother. I want to be his wife."
2. "If I push any harder, he'll just get better at hiding things."
3. "I'm not going to trick the man into a doctor's office. That's not a
   marriage."
4. "I refuse to make learning CPR my retirement plan."
5. "We're in our sixties. 'Wait and see' — wait for WHAT?"

## Primary Transformation

**If a Genie Snapped Their Fingers:**
Frank *wants* to know. He watches the video himself — better, he brings it
to HER. A doctor-shaped authority says the things she's been saying for
years, and Frank listens, because it isn't coming from her. There are real
numbers instead of her 1 a.m. imagination, a plan instead of a vigil, and
the fear finally has a shape small enough to act on. She sleeps through the
night. The health project becomes something they do together — the morning
walks, the meals — instead of something she does *to* him.

**How It Would Affect Her Closest Relationships:**
1. **Frank:** Peer-to-peer warmth returns. She gets to be interesting to
   him again instead of worried about him. The dinner table talks about
   Portugal, not portions.
2. **The kids:** They see their parents handling it as a unit and stand
   down. Calls go back to being about the grandchild.
3. **Friends:** She becomes the quiet resource — "who's that heart doctor
   you found?" — the one who knew where to look, not the one who nagged.
4. **Herself:** With the vigilance channel closed, she books her own
   overdue appointments. She stops rehearsing widowhood. (Vain, never
   admitted: she wants credit — for him to someday say "you were right,
   and you saved my life." She'd settle for him just watching the video.)

## Market Specifics

**What Does This Market Hinge Its Success On?**
His buy-in. Everything she wants sits behind the one thing she cannot
purchase, schedule, or cook: the moment Frank engages voluntarily. This is
the strategic center of the Diane campaign — **the product she is
"buying" is a bridge**: a credible, forwardable, pride-safe way for the
message to reach him from someone who isn't her.

**What Does She Have to Give Up By Giving Up the Problem?**
The identity of the vigilant one. Worry has been her proof of love and her
job title for a decade — if he engages, she loses the moral high ground,
the control of the project, and the quiet martyrdom of "nobody knows how
much I carry." Deeper: ambiguity has been shelter. As long as nobody looks,
there's no bad number. If he finally gets tested, it becomes real — and
part of her is more afraid of the answer than of the silence.

**Who Does She Blame?**
His stubbornness and the male-pride culture of his generation; the
7-minute-visit doctors who reassure instead of look; a system that told
him "you're fine" so many times it armed him against her; the American
food environment; and his family's fatalism — "the men in his family just
die at 65, like it's a schedule."

**Top 5 Biggest Objections:**
1. "He'll never watch it." (The objection. Every creative and landing
   decision must answer it.)
2. "Another YouTube doctor? How do I know this one's real?" (Answer:
   Hopkins, 40 years, lifestyle-first, not anti-medicine — the credibility
   stack matters more for Diane than for Frank.)
3. "His own doctor isn't worried. Who am I to overrule a doctor?"
4. "If he finds out I've been researching him, it's a fight."
5. "What if we look and it's bad — and it breaks him?" (The "mentally
   destroyed me" fear, held on someone else's behalf.)

## Sub-Markets (segmentation)

**1. The Sentinel Wife (Diane prime)** — 55–68, married 30+ years.
Watched his father's line repeat; her fear has a date on it. Highest
intent, highest emotional charge, most constrained by his pride. Wants a
bridge, not a lecture. Reached by night-researcher and tried-everything
identification. The core of the parallel campaign.

**2. The Long-Distance Daughter/Son** — 32–48, researching Dad from
another city. Role-reversal grief before the fact; operates through the
family group chat and guilt about distance. Wants something forwardable
that doesn't sound like "Dad, I think you're dying." Distinct creative
voice (Role Reversal concept) and distinct placements (skews younger —
Instagram-weighted).

**3. The Already-Scarred** — 60–75, buried a husband, brother, or close
friend's husband. Retroactive urgency: "I wish I'd known" energy aimed at
the men still living — second husband, son, friends' husbands. The
market's natural evangelist; highest share rate, lower click intent.
Bridges Diane's world back to the proven #3 bereavement block.

**4. The Health-Partner Wife** — 55–70, her Frank is compliant but
passive; she is the household's executive function. Lower fear, higher
implementation appetite: wants plans, protocols, shopping lists for two.
Likely the best *program buyer* of the four even though her ad-layer
identification is calmer.

**5. The Clinical Insider** — 55–70, retired nurse / caregiver / med-
adjacent. Knows enough to be properly scared and to smell hype instantly.
Hardest to win, most valuable socially (her forward carries clinical
authority inside her circle). Credentials-first messaging; zero tolerance
for supplement-hype tone.

## Parallel Diane Campaign — structure

**Separation:** Diane runs as her own campaign tree, fully parallel to the
Frank campaigns — separate budgets, separate learning, no shared fate.
(Quarantine logic; the secrecy theme demands it anyway.)

**What the ad layer sells:** identification only, as ever — but the
persona clarifies WHICH identifications. New candidate confession lines
the framework surfaced, each needing its own block-control test before any
scaling (all first person, viewer's own behavior, at-risk man in third
person):

- "I made the appointment. He rescheduled it twice." *(tried-and-failed —
  likely the broadest)*
- "The watch was $400. He uses it to check the weather." *(humor-warm
  identification; high share potential)*
- "I don't want to be his mother. I want to be his wife." *(the deepest
  cut; test carefully — relationship-role confession, still compliant)*
- "I know more about heart disease at 1 a.m. than I ever wanted to."
- Already built (Pain Point #21 doc): the night-search, the 47 tabs, the
  forwarder, the role reversal, NOT FOR ME FOR HIM.

**What the destination must do (Diane-specific landing/bridge):**
1. Narrator first person, acknowledging the on-behalf viewer without
   second-person condition talk: "About half the people who watch my
   videos aren't watching for themselves."
2. Answer objection #1 structurally: the page's job is to arm the
   forward, not just the click — the video must be framed as something
   easy to hand to him ("worth ten minutes" energy, pride-safe, not
   scary).
3. Answer objection #2 fast: credentials above the fold (Hopkins, 40
   years, lifestyle-first, medications-when-needed) — Diane vets harder
   than Frank.
4. Contain fear (calm-after-the-scare): process language, never doom.

**Measurement:** For Diane, the share/send is a primary success event, not
a vanity metric — her conversion IS the forward. Track link CTR against
block controls as always, but also share rate and outbound sends per
impression; a Diane ad with average CTR and exceptional shares is working
exactly as designed. Ultimate CAC credit will lag and mis-attribute (Frank
converts later, on a device she handed him) — expect view-through and
household-level effects, and judge this campaign patiently.

**Do-not-migrate warning:** The fears, genie outcomes, and hurtful quotes
in this document are research, not copy. Nothing above passes to an ad
until it is first person, identification-only, outcome-free, and imagery-
safe — same gate as everything else.
